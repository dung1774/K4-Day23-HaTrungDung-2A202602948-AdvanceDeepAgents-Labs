# Video and Multimodal Generation: A Survey

## Abstract
Video generation has developed from short, frame-oriented synthesis into systems that represent video as a temporally structured signal and increasingly generate sound jointly with images. This survey synthesizes retrieved evidence on model representations, text-to-video quality and compositional evaluation, joint audio-video architectures, and safety evaluation and deployment. The evidence supports a nuanced picture: latent and patch-based representations offer flexible handling of duration and resolution, while temporal coherence, prompt adherence, cross-modal synchronization, and safety remain distinct problems that require separate assessment. Benchmarks contribute useful decompositions but do not provide a single universal score. Claims about recent systems are often author-reported, and results across papers cannot be compared without common evaluation conditions.

## Scope and evidence approach
This survey draws on three independently gathered research note sets and uses primary papers, a provider technical report/system card, and a hosted paper record as source families. The synthesis distinguishes direct descriptions and benchmark findings from author-reported performance claims. Spot checks verified the VBench dimensionality and SyncFlow architecture claims; the checks were inconclusive on specific Sora limitations, the exact T2VSafetyBench prompt count, and Sora 2 provenance disclosures. Those details are therefore either omitted or qualified rather than asserted as settled facts.

## 1. Video representations and generation
Video generation must model both visual content and its evolution through time. The broad video-diffusion literature surveys architectures, temporal dynamics, training, evaluation, and applications, establishing diffusion as a major method family without implying that it is the only viable approach [1]. OpenAI's Sora report describes a text-conditional diffusion transformer trained on videos and images, with visual inputs compressed to a latent representation and represented using spacetime patches [2]. The report presents this representation as supporting varied durations, resolutions, and aspect ratios. These are provider-reported system characteristics, not an independently reproduced comparison.

Such representations make video a sequence-level generation problem rather than a collection of unrelated still images. In practice, temporal identity, smooth motion, and stable scene relationships must be evaluated alongside per-frame appearance. The Sora report itself cautions against interpreting generated video as reliable physical simulation: its discussion acknowledges limitations in physical interactions and longer-duration coherence [2]. More broadly, scalable representation does not by itself establish persistent object identity, causal understanding, or robust long-horizon dynamics.

## 2. Evaluation: quality, alignment, and composition
A central methodological point is that “video quality” is not one property. VBench decomposes assessment into 16 dimensions, including subject identity consistency, motion smoothness, temporal flicker, and spatial relationships. It also distinguishes visual quality from consistency with conditioning and uses human preference annotations to assess how automated evaluation relates to human perception [3]. This multidimensional framing is important because an aggregate score can obscure a model's particular strengths or failures.

Other evaluation efforts target narrower deficiencies. T2VScore is presented as combining text-video alignment with video quality, while T2V-CompBench focuses on compositional prompt-following, including attributes, spatial relations, action binding, interactions, and numeracy [4][5]. These goals complement broad quality suites: a visually polished clip may still omit a requested action or misplace objects, while a prompt-aligned clip may contain artifacts or unstable motion. The available records identify these benchmark aims but do not provide enough common quantitative evidence to rank the methods or establish equivalence among their scores.

Evaluation should therefore report disaggregated measures, prompt and dataset construction, human assessment procedures, and failure cases. Cross-paper comparisons are especially fragile when models are tested with different prompts, sampling settings, resolutions, or metric implementations. The sources reviewed here support the need for complementary metrics, not a definitive universal evaluation protocol.

## 3. Joint audio-video generation
Multimodal generation extends the task from visual temporal coherence to synchronization between modalities. A video may depict an event at one time while its audio indicates another; producing plausible sound and plausible images separately does not guarantee their correspondence. Recent joint systems address this with modality-specific streams and cross-modal interaction.

SyncFlow proposes a dual-diffusion-transformer with separate audio and video towers, followed by information fusion and staged training: modalities are learned separately before joint fine-tuning [6]. Its paper reports improvements in audio quality and audio-visual correspondence over its selected baselines. These are results reported by the authors under their experimental setup, not independent evidence of general superiority. JavisDiT similarly describes hierarchical, global and fine-grained spatio-temporal priors to guide event timing and location across audio and video; the paper reports synchronization improvements on its benchmarks and introduces a captioned sounding-video dataset [7].

A newer system description, LTX-2, specifies asymmetric video and audio streams connected by bidirectional cross-attention and claims synchronized speech and environmental sound generation [8]. The record also reports comparative quality claims, which should be treated cautiously absent a shared independent evaluation. Other records describe unified diffusion-transformer or mixture-of-experts approaches, but their available summaries are insufficient for quantitative comparison [9][10]. Across these designs, the architectural trade-off is between specialized modality processing and sufficiently rich interaction to align events. A meaningful benchmark should measure not only audio and video quality separately, but also temporal correspondence, event localization, and prompt adherence across both modalities.

## 4. Safety and deployment
Safety in video generation is intrinsically temporal and contextual. T2VSafetyBench defines multiple risk aspects, including cases in which a sequence can be harmful even when individual frames appear innocuous; it evaluates systems using prompt sets that include adversarial examples [11]. The source reports trade-offs among safety aspects and usability, but benchmark outcomes are bounded by the selected systems, prompts, and assessment methods. It should not be interpreted as a deployment guarantee.

SAFESORA contributes human preference data pairing prompts and generated videos, distinguishing helpfulness from harmlessness, and proposes moderation that considers prompt and video together [12]. Its reported agreement with human judgments is tied to its test set and annotation design. Together, these works motivate multimodal and sequence-aware safeguards: frame-only checks can miss changes over time, and video-only moderation can miss prompt context.

The Sora 2 system card describes iterative deployment controls, input and output scanning, classifiers, red-teaming, and provenance measures [13]. These are provider disclosures rather than independent proof of effectiveness. The evidence available to the citation spot-check did not verify specific C2PA or watermark claims or a precise statement about contextual-deception detection, so those particulars are not treated here as confirmed. Safety consequently remains a lifecycle concern involving model training, input controls, output review, provenance, monitoring, and response to misuse—not a property demonstrated by a single benchmark.

## 5. Open challenges and research priorities
First, temporal consistency needs evaluation at multiple time scales: local motion smoothness, persistent identities, event ordering, and long-range narrative coherence are related but separable. Second, multimodal benchmarks should make synchronization measurable and reproducible, rather than infer it from separate audio and video scores. Third, compositional evaluation should test interactions among attributes, actions, and spatial relations under diverse prompt formulations. Fourth, safety evaluation should include temporal and contextual risks, adversarial inputs, and human review, while reporting the limits imposed by dataset coverage and automated judges. Finally, system papers should distinguish model capability from deployment behavior and disclose enough evaluation detail for independent reproduction.

## Conclusion
Video and multimodal generation is best understood as a set of coupled but distinct challenges: visual synthesis, temporal organization, instruction following, cross-modal synchronization, and safety. Patch/latent representations and joint audio-video architectures offer promising design patterns, while multidimensional benchmarks expose weaknesses that a single quality score would hide. Current evidence supports careful optimism, not claims of solved temporal understanding or reliable safety. Progress depends on stronger shared evaluations, transparent reporting, and independent validation of system claims.
